NetBBS door game proposal

Fair Winds

Read the wind. Carry the post. Finish the map.

A balloon cannot steer. It can only climb or sink, and every altitude blows a different way. Fair Winds is a turn-based navigation game built on that one fact. It is set on a continent that is unique to each BBS, starts out blank, and is charted by the node's callers together. Everyone flies the same weather, and the weather follows the real calendar.

Every mockup on this page is drawn by script onto a strict character grid: one glyph, one foreground and one background colour per cell, with half blocks used as pixels. Nothing shown needs more than a truecolor UTF-8 terminal can display. Frame rates and bandwidth still have to be measured on real transports.

Why this game

The mechanic is new to doors. Steering by altitude is how real balloons navigate, and it makes a clean turn-based puzzle: two keys, visible consequences, and a lot to learn. I know of no door game built on it, and few games of any kind.

Truecolor is the playfield. A wind field over shaded relief only reads with thousands of colours. The game's best pictures are skies: dusk, storms, aurora. Gradients like these are what a 16-colour door could never draw.

Wide screens show more world. Extra columns become more map and more instruments, so a wide terminal is a real advantage and a narrow one still plays.

It fills the gap in the NetBBS line-up. None of the current doors is cooperative, and none is calm.

Four loops, one inside the next

Seconds
The turn
One turn is fifteen minutes of flight. Look at where each layer would take you, then climb, sink or hold. Climbing burns fuel, sinking is free, holding costs a little. The balloon drifts and the weather moves one step.
5 to 10 minutes
The flying day
Launch at dawn, be down by dusk: 30 to 50 turns. Deliver mail, chart blank map, photograph what you find. Land at a station or camp in the wild. Where you land is where the next day starts.
28 world days
The season
Each 28-day season brings a weather regime and a goal for the whole node, such as opening a mountain pass with a chain of mooring masts. A long-distance race fills the last week. Medals close it.
Months
The continent
The chart grows from blank to complete. The almanac fills with named winds. The ring of stations closes in on the Gyre, the standing storm at the centre that nobody has entered.

One flying day, start to finish

  1. Briefing. Where you are, today's weather in two lines, what is in your basket.
  2. Launch. Step through the layers with W and S. The tracks redraw before you commit.
  3. Fly. Fuel, ballast and daylight run down while you work toward the destination or off into blank map.
  4. Land. A full-screen scene, then the tally: distance, map charted, mail delivered, and what your observations did for other callers' forecasts.
  5. Sign the guestbook. Stay the night, or launch again if daylight remains.

Paced by the calendar, never by the call. A new world day opens at the node's midnight. A pilot flies each world day once, and can never fly a day that has not opened. Up to three opened days wait, so a missed day costs nothing. Connecting again changes nothing: a flight is saved every turn and resumes exactly where it stopped, and every outcome is fixed by the world's seed, so hanging up rerolls nothing.

There is always something to fly. Trials are challenge flights with medals and unlimited attempts. The Daily Drift is one short course, the same for everyone on the node, with one scored attempt per world day and unlimited practice.

How it plays

Flying

  • Six layers from the surface to the jet stream, each with its own wind. Mountains block the low ones, so a range has to be climbed over or threaded through a gap.
  • Fuel pays for climbing and holding. Ballast is the emergency: jettison a sack for an instant free climb, with no refill until a station. A pibal is a pilot balloon that reads every layer above you exactly, once.
  • Midday sun gives free lift. Night doubles fuel use and hides the ground, so dusk is a real deadline unless a lit mast is in reach.
  • Hazards are weather: squalls, icing up high, rotor behind ridges, dawn fog, surface wind too strong to land in.

Weather

The weather is a pure function of the node's seed and the real date: prevailing winds per layer, a few highs and lows that take days to cross, sea breezes, valley winds, and noise. Nothing runs while nobody is playing. When a caller connects, the door computes today.

Everyone flying today gets the same day. A low that parks over the Kettle Range stays there until Thursday for all callers, which gives a message board something to talk about.

The forecast is the same function with error added. The error grows with time and with distance from the nearest weather station, and stations are planted by players.

Failure

No career ends. Run out of fuel and you come down where you are. Over land you are stranded until you walk out, which costs two days, or until another caller flies supplies in. Over water the packet boat picks you up and the mail is lost. The cost is time and reputation.

Progress

  • Licence, from Fledgling to Commodore of the Post, earned by distance, deliveries and clean landings.
  • The balloon: a bigger envelope, a better burner, instruments that lengthen the forecast, night gear, oxygen for the jet layer, and late on a small engine that can crab across the wind. You choose the envelope's colours and pattern, and other callers see them on their maps.
  • The almanac: fly a recurring wind often enough and it is entered under your name. From then on every caller sees it in the forecast.
  • Wonders: forty rare sights, each a full-screen picture. Stamps from every town.

A world the callers build

Shared work

  • Charting. Flying over blank map fills it in for everyone. The first caller to chart a peak, lake or pass names it. Names are composed from the game's word lists, so nothing needs moderating.
  • Stations. Plant one where you land. It sharpens the forecast around it for every caller. It needs resupply now and then or it lapses.
  • Masts. Several callers fly materials in to raise a station into a mooring mast: night landings, fuel, a post office. This is how the frontier moves.
  • Rescue. Stranded callers show on the chart. Fly supplies in and both of you are rewarded.
  • Guestbooks. One line per visit at each station.

The game has to work for a single caller on a quiet node. Other callers make everything faster. They never gate it.

The Gyre

At the heart of every continent stands a permanent storm. Its winds get faster and stranger toward the centre, the forecast fails there, and nothing can be built inside it. Seasons push the ring of masts inward.

Reaching the eye takes a late-game balloon, a community's worth of stations and a real understanding of spiral winds. The first caller on a node to do it is recorded for good. What is in the eye is the game's one secret.

No rivals

There is no combat and no way to harm another caller. Competition is in the standings: the Daily Drift, distance flown, features named, the season race.

Any terminal

Play never depends on colour alone. Wind always has a direction glyph, a compass point and a number. The same turn can be played on a classic 80 × 24 terminal in 16 colours, and callers with motion switched off get a still streak pattern.

No companion service is needed. The world is asynchronous, and the weather is a function of the clock.

What to test before building

  1. Is choosing a layer fun for forty turns? Build the wind model and a plain-text flight first, with no art. If reading three tracks and picking one does not hold up, nothing else matters.
  2. Render budget. Animated wind over a 118 × 38 map changes several hundred cells a frame. Measure bytes and CPU over SSH, Telnet and the browser terminal against the 300 CPU-second default. The fallback is animation in short bursts between turns.
  3. The quiet node. Check that one caller alone can make steady progress, and that three callers feel like a community.
  4. Legibility at 80 × 24. The layer table has to carry the decision when the map cannot.
  5. The calendar window. Three waiting days is a first guess.

Set aside

A deep-sea descent with a real light engine: a lamp cone, bioluminescence, colour fading with depth. It would look superb, but dive, collect and upgrade is a well-worn loop.

Shortwave listening on a truecolor waterfall display, hunting and decoding stations. Too close in feel to War Dialer's scanning.
